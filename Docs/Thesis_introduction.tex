\chapter{Introduction}

Land-cover change is a significant cause of greenhouse gas emissions \parencite{masolele2021}. Monitoring
Landcover- change is mandated by international agreements, for participating countries \parencite{voigt2025}, such as the REDD+
(Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation) programme of the United Nations Framework
Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). This programme requires developing countries to report changes in land-cover and offers
results-based payments to countries that demonstrate a verified reduction in carbon emissions resulting from the land-cover change.
%Oto what AI is doing here now to determine to the maatschappelijke relevantie 
To verify this reduction, Sample-Based Area Estimation (SBAE) is used. SBAE is
a statistical method that estimates the area of land-cover classes from a
probability sample of random observations \parencite{jonckheere2024}. For
land-cover change, the area of change is inferred from the sample points
observed to change class between dates.

Pixel counting from a classified map gives biased area estimates, as omission
and commission errors do not cancel out \parencite{olofsson2014}.
SBAE avoids this bias by estimating area from reference labels at randomly
selected sample points, each weighted by the area it represents \parencite{olofsson2014}.
Furthermore, SBAE quantifies the uncertainty of the area estimate as a confidence
interval, which pixel counting cannot provide \parencite{olofsson2014}. Reporting
this uncertainty is required under the UNFCCC \parencite{ipcc2019}, and higher
uncertainty can reduce REDD+ results-based payments \parencite{jonckheere2024}.

Uncertainty should therefore be kept as low as possible. One way to
achieve this is to increase the number of sample points. As land-cover change is a 
rare event, estimating it precisely requires more samples \parencite{pagliarella2018}.

Currently, sample points are labelled by human annotators using
high-resolution imagery such as Google Earth \parencite{olofsson2014}.
This is time-consuming, and for the large samples needed to estimate rare
change it is often not feasible within reporting deadlines
\parencite{jonckheere2024}. In this thesis, annotation costs therefore refer to the
time annotators spend labelling the sample points of an SBAE.
Machine-learning approaches, in particular deep learning, can reduce this
burden by labelling part of the sample automatically, so that a sufficient
number of sample points is reached in less time \parencite{jonckheere2024}.

Deep learning has the potential to outperform traditional pixel-based
classifiers, as it captures both spatial and temporal patterns more effectively
\parencite{masolele2021}. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have successfully
been used to classify deforestation in satellite imagery \parencite{pisl2024}.
Vision Transformers (ViTs), which adapt the transformer architecture from
language processing to images, were introduced as an alternative to CNNs and
outperformed them in classification tasks when pretrained on large datasets
\parencite{dosovitskiy2021}. \textcite{kaselimi2022} further showed that ViTs
are better equipped to detect rare events such as land cover change. However,
the effect of using ViTs to classify land cover change at sample points has not
yet been investigated.

\textcite{tsendbazar2021} showed that change-detection tools such as Breaks For
Additive Season and Trend (BFAST) can reliably identify stable classes, so that
those sample points can be reused for later estimations. However, BFAST does not
take neighbouring pixels into account; it only considers the values of a single
pixel over time. A ViT model, by contrast, also considers the surrounding
spatial context. This additional context around the sample points could help to
also predict the dynamic classes, where BFAST could not provide such
information.

One promising type of such models is the foundation model. Foundation models are
pretrained in a self-supervised way on large amounts of broad, unlabelled data,
through which they learn general patterns and relationships in that data
\parencite{szwarcman2025}. This thesis uses a geospatial foundation model: a
foundation model pretrained on large amounts of satellite imagery. It is based
on the ViT architecture and was pretrained by reconstructing masked patches of
satellite images \parencite{szwarcman2025}. Because of these previously learned
patterns, geospatial foundation models can outperform deep learning models
trained from scratch, such as CNNs \parencite{szwarcman2025, simumba2025}, and
require less labelled data to achieve accurate predictions \parencite{szwarcman2025}.

The labelled data can be used to fine-tune the encoder. Fine-tuning adapts
the pretrained encoder to the specific task and data, in this case sample point
classification. Fine-tuning slightly adjusts some of the encoder's weights. This
changes the embeddings it produces. The embeddings are the output of the
encoder, it creates the patterns and relationships the model found in the data.
A classification head is added to the model architecture, since the model is
made for image reconstruction \parencite{szwarcman2025}, but needs to classify
sample points. These adjusted embeddings help the classification head predict
classes better \parencite{simumba2025}.

Even so, the fine-tuned model will not classify every sample point correctly,
and incorrect model labels can bias the area estimate, which decreases the overal quality of the data.
A human interpreter can indicate when they are unsure about a point; for a
model, this has to be derived from its output in the form of a confidence
score. In this thesis, confidence is expressed as margin: the difference
between the probabilities of the two most likely classes \parencite{valle2023}.
A large margin indicates that the model clearly prefers one class, while a
small margin indicates doubt between the top two predicted classes. It should
therefore be checked whether the confidence reflects the accuracy, meaning that
sample points with a higher confidence are more often classified correctly
\parencite{geifman2017}. If so, the confidence can be used to determine which
points still require (less) human interpretation, reducing the annotation costs.

Human interpretation is subjective, as interpreters may assign different
labels to the same sample point \parencite{pengra2020, gfoi2018}. Such errors
can bias area estimates, and variability among interpreters increases their
uncertainty \parencite{mcroberts2018, jonckheere2024}. A model applies the
same criteria to every sample point.

Recent benchmarking efforts for foundation models have focused on standardising
accuracy evaluation across different geospatial tasks and datasets
\parencite{simumba2025}. To date, no research has examined the potential of deep
learning foundation models to reduce the annotation costs of sample point
classification.

This research therefore differs from previous work in that it focuses on
optimising the classification of sample points rather than on producing a land
cover change map. This is done by fine-tuning a foundation model to classify
land cover change at SBAE sample points and by quantifying the confidence of the
model.

The aim of this research is to assess whether a fine-tuned foundation model can
take over part of the human annotation in SBAE without lowering label quality.
